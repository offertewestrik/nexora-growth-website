Waarom dit werkt

The problem with generic calculators

Search “inground pool cost” from anywhere in the United States and you will find a dozen calculators. They are useful to the homeowner and almost useless to you. They quote a wide national range that does not reflect what a gunite build costs in Phoenix versus a fiberglass drop-in outside Tampa. Then they sell the lead, sometimes to you, sometimes to three of your competitors at once.

The homeowner arrives at your door with a number in their head that came from someone else's spreadsheet. Correcting that number is the first conversation you have, and it is a bad one to start with.

What changes when the calculator is yours

You stay in control of how the estimate is framed. Most builders show a range rather than a single figure, with a clear line that the final price depends on site conditions and a survey. That keeps the number honest and keeps you out of a conversation about a quote you never gave.

Questions builders ask

How accurate is the estimate?

As accurate as the numbers you give it. It is built from your cost sheet, so it is as close as any figure can be before someone has seen the site. Most builders present it as a range and state plainly that the final price follows a survey, that is honest and it holds up.

Will it undercut my quoting?

It should not, and that is a matter of how you set it. The estimate is a filter, not a quote. Builders who set it slightly conservative find it protects them: the homeowner who balks at the estimate was never going to sign at the real price.

Can I hide the price and just collect the design?

Yes. Some builders run it without a visible estimate and use it purely as a specification tool. You still get the full design in the request. In our experience the price is what makes people finish it, but the choice is yours.

Does it handle regional cost differences?

Yes, because you set the numbers. If you build in two markets with different labor rates, that can be reflected in the pricing table.
